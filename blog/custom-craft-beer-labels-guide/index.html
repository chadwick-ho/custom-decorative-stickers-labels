Craft Beer Labels: Connect the Bottle and Can Proofs

Published September 26, 2026. Updated October 27, 2026.

By ZC Labels · B2B procurement guide · Sources and editorial approach

In this guide
  1. Put two containers beside the same approved identity
  2. Make the package reference visible on the proof
  3. Keep the shared design and physical fit in separate approvals
  4. Record the two application stages
  5. Compare the order as a complete pair

The brewery has approved one identity for a beer that will appear in cans and bottles. The supplier receives a single artwork file and a combined quantity. That is not yet a complete label order: the can and bottle need their own physical references, cut boundaries and application conditions, even when the front artwork is clearly related.

Start with one approved product identity, then identify the two package-specific proofs. Compare each proof with its actual container before the order is released. A shared visual system can keep the beer recognizable without assuming that a bottle label fits a can.

Put two containers beside the same approved identity

The product owner supplies the approved brand, beer name and relevant wording for each destination. List which packages actually use that identity and the number of labels required for each one. If only the can is ready, mark the bottle version pending rather than treating a copied design as approved.

Use the actual can and bottle references. Measure the usable label area on each and note features that the proposed label must avoid. A can may use a partial panel or a wrap; a bottle may use front and back pieces or another arrangement. Do not infer the selected format from a generic product image or from the container's nominal volume.

The illustration shows a fictional beer identity on two packages and separate proofs. It explains the relationship between shared identity and distinct physical references. It does not represent a brewery customer, an approved dieline or a performance result.

Make the package reference visible on the proof

Give each package version a clear name in the release record. Include the container reference, finished label dimensions, artwork revision and quantity. Where there is more than one physical piece, such as a front and back bottle set, list those pieces separately and show the owner-approved combination.

Release reference What the buyer records What it does not establish
Product identity Owner-approved name and wording Fit on either container
Can proof Can reference, cut boundary and quantity Bottle label placement
Bottle proof Bottle reference, pieces and quantity Can wrap direction or join
Applied reference Specific package, construction and conditions Performance for an unrepresented package

A full-size paper mockup can show whether the proposed front view, join and usable panel make sense on the real package. It is an inexpensive way to catch a footprint question before requesting a construction sample. It does not approve adhesive contact, print protection or resistance to condensation.

For the dedicated can decision, use the can wrap-join guide. It addresses the leading edge and where a wrap finishes. This page coordinates the bottle and can proofs; it should not replace the actual can orientation review.

Keep the shared design and physical fit in separate approvals

The brand owner may want the same central illustration and strong product name on both packs. The cut boundary may nevertheless change because a bottle body, shoulder and mold features are not the same as a can wall and its transitions. Check the package-specific reference rather than stretching one flat proof until it looks approximately right.

If approved wording does not fit the selected panel, return the problem to the responsible product owner and packaging team. They decide the permitted content and placement for the destination; the printer can flag fit and readability issues but should not invent alcohol statements, government warnings or other regulated copy. A paper prototype at the final size supports a physical review; it is not legal approval.

The wine label change guide covers a different task: controlling changed fields on front and back artwork. Use it for the file-change method, while keeping beer content approval with the appropriate owner.

Record the two application stages

Ask when each container is labeled and describe its surface condition at that point. One route may apply labels to dry containers while another encounters colder or more humid conditions. Keep the actual application stage distinct from later refrigeration, transport or service. Neither a shiny finish nor a generic “waterproof” description confirms all stages.

If a material sample is agreed, identify its complete construction and the package, application and handling sequence it represents. Agree on the observations and acceptance with the responsible parties. A sample applied to one dry package cannot prove a different cold or wet application route. Do not infer an actual test, order, factory condition or service life from the illustration.

Where the two packages need different constructions, record those differences beside the shared artwork reference. Where the supplier proposes the same construction, ask which conditions its evidence covers for each container. That distinction makes the comparison useful without forcing the buyer to choose two materials merely because there are two packages.

Compare the order as a complete pair

A shared base design can reduce duplicated design work, but two physical proofs, two container checks and potentially different supply instructions still need attention. Conversely, approving one package first may let its production discussion proceed while the other remains open, if the supplier and brewery agree the scope. Compare the handling and version-control work before treating either route as automatically faster or cheaper.

Keep the can proof, bottle proof, approved copy, quantities and any outstanding package questions together. A later can supplier change or bottle mold change may reopen the respective fit decision without invalidating every part of the shared brand design. A new beer name or market version can also change the content that must be approved.

For a craft beer label review, send photos and dimensions of both containers, the proposed can and bottle label positions, owner-approved artwork and wording, application conditions for each package, quantities by design and destination. The custom roll label page supports the supply-format discussion. These references let the supplier separate common artwork from package-specific fit and construction questions.
