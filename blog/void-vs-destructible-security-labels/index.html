VOID vs Destructible Labels: Define the Receiver’s Evidence

Published 2026-10-16. Updated October 24, 2026.

By ZC Labels · B2B procurement guide · Sources and editorial approach

In this guide
  1. Test the Seal on the Real Closure
  2. Design for Inspection, Not Decoration Alone
  3. Write the evidence rule before comparing samples
  4. Keep the intact and changed states connected
  5. Check whether a receiver can recognize the intended change
  6. Give exceptions a destination

The word "security" sounds reassuring on a quote sheet. It tells me very little about what the receiver will actually see. Imagine a small electronics carton: the closing flap lifts, and the warehouse needs to decide whether the carton has been opened. A label that looks impressive while intact but peels away without a useful trace has missed its job.

I would start by asking what evidence the buyer expects after an attempted lift. A VOID-style construction aims to leave a visible transfer message or pattern. A destructible construction aims to fragment rather than come away in one neat piece. Neither description is a guarantee on every box. The face stock, adhesive, surface coating, time after application and opening motion work together.

Test the Seal on the Real Closure

Use the production carton or container, not only a supplier's demonstration sheet. Apply samples across the exact flap or cap position. Try an ordinary opening, a slow corner lift and handling after the pack has spent time in its expected storage environment. Photograph the intact seal and the attempted opening. Decide whether the residue or fragment is obvious to a person checking many packs quickly.

A heavily coated carton, textured paper or dusty plastic may behave differently from smooth test film. If a seal bridges two moving panels, check whether normal shipping flex makes it fail before the customer opens the pack. That is a false alarm, and repeated false alarms make any evidence system less useful.

Design for Inspection, Not Decoration Alone

Print a complete original mark, a clear inspection cue and, where useful, an approved batch or serial field. Do not rely on tiny security wording that disappears at the final size. Keep the artwork away from the engineered evidence area unless the label supplier confirms the effect remains visible. A box may need a separate product label and a smaller security seal instead of forcing both jobs into one crowded sticker.

The product owner should define the inspection rule: what counts as opened, who photographs the result and what happens to an exception. A tamper-evident label cannot establish chain of custody by itself, and it should not be marketed as tamper-proof.

  1. Send the actual packed carton and closure location
  2. Describe the evidence the receiver must notice
  3. Trial both constructions on the real surface
  4. Check shipping flex and intended storage
  5. Approve visible artwork at actual size
  6. Document the inspection decision

Write the evidence rule before comparing samples

Ask the receiving owner what observation should lead to acceptance, a hold or further review. Describe the intact state, the visible change expected after the agreed opening attempt and how staff will inspect it. A transfer pattern and a fragmented face can both be visible, but they communicate different physical changes. The buyer needs a rule that relates the chosen construction to the actual receiving task.

The 3M tamper-evident material description identifies destructible facestocks and options with transfer messages or patterns. These are manufacturer construction examples. Their product properties, application approvals and performance claims do not transfer to another label simply because its quote uses the same family name.

Keep the intact and changed states connected

Identify the sample construction, artwork, dimensions, package surface and closure position. Photograph the intact sample before the owner's agreed observation sequence, then retain the changed sample with the same reference. Without that connection, a dramatic photograph of fragments says little about what was supplied or how it behaved on this package.

Include the storage and normal handling stages relevant to the project. If an intact seal changes during ordinary movement, record that separately from the intended opening observation. The buyer should not have to choose between missing a meaningful change and treating routine package flex as evidence of interference. The parties need to decide which observations the proposed construction can support.

Check whether a receiver can recognize the intended change

Inspect at the actual working distance and with the access available at receipt. A tiny transfer pattern hidden beneath a flap can be technically visible in a close-up yet unhelpful in the normal inspection. Original artwork and an inspection cue should leave the engineered evidence area understandable. Do not cover it with decoration unless the proposed construction supplier confirms the intended effect is preserved.

An illustrative comparison could show one box with a transfer pattern and another with fragmented label pieces. It does not prove either construction protects every opening route. The package owner should consider the whole closure and inspection process, including exceptions, without advertising a sticker as tamper-proof or as a chain-of-custody system.

Give exceptions a destination

Name who reviews a damaged, missing or ambiguous seal and what record that person needs. A hold route is more useful than asking a receiver to guess whether a partly lifted edge is acceptable. If a seal must be replaced during authorized rework, define how that rework is recorded and which new seal becomes the current reference. Do not let an unexplained replacement erase the package history.

For the adjacent decision, see tamper evident labels guide and jar lid tamper seals vs shrink bands.

Send the actual closure and surface, inspection requirement, handling route, dimensions, artwork and quantities through the security-label enquiry. State the visible evidence the receiver needs, rather than only asking for the strongest seal. The result should be a bounded construction and observation proposal; final acceptance belongs to the responsible packaging owner.
